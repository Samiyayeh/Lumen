# Detection of Appliance-Level Abnormal Energy Consumption in Buildings Using Autoencoders and Micro-moments

**Category:** [[Related Study international C2]]

**Yassine Himeur, Abdullah Alsalemi, Faycal Bensaali, and Abbes Amira. 2022. Detection of Appliance-Level Abnormal Energy Consumption in Buildings Using Autoencoders and Micro-moments. In *Proceedings of the 5th International Conference on Big Data and Internet of Things (BDIoT 2021)* (Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems, Vol. 488). Springer, 185–197. DOI:https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-07969-6_14**

- **Summary:** Himeur et al. (2022) developed and evaluated an appliance-level anomaly detection and behavioral energy efficiency framework engineered as part of the $(EM)^3$ project at Qatar University; the system pairs IoT smart plug hardware combining ESP-WROOM-32 microcontrollers (0.16s processing speed, 3.19s communication latency, $10 unit cost) and intrusive current sensors (HLW8012 / ACS712 Hall-effect sensors measuring up to 20A) with environmental monitoring (AM312 motion, DHT22 temperature/humidity, TSL2591 luminosity) transmitting to a CouchDB backend, where the methodology extracts time-domain micro-moment features—specifically Device Standby Power Consumption (DSPC) and operational duration vectors—and utilizes deep autoencoder neural networks alongside ensemble decision bagging trees (DBT achieving 98.79% accuracy and 98.52% F1-score) to detect abnormal appliance power draw and idle equipment waste.

- **Relevance:** This study directly informs and justifies the anomaly detection logic and ghost load identification objective of the proposed system by demonstrating that monitoring device standby power consumption (DSPC) and inactive duration metrics enables precise isolation of idle energy waste and equipment left powered on without active user occupancy. However, the study relies on intrusive, individual smart plugs (HLW8012 / ACS712) deployed at single wall-socket outlets and computationally demanding deep autoencoder neural networks. Outfitting every classroom appliance or workstation with custom intrusive smart plugs is financially and physically cost-prohibitive for campus-wide university scaling. Furthermore, its anomaly detection models operate at the isolated appliance level without aggregated branch-circuit sub-panel monitoring, and its context evaluation relies on localized PIR motion sensors rather than integrating institutional academic class timetables or room reservation schedules, rendering it unable to detect unscheduled off-hours ghost consumption across broader departmental zones. The proposed system resolves these boundaries by replacing costly plug-level intrusive devices with non-invasive split-core current transformer (CT) clamp sensors installed directly inside department distribution sub-panels, and replacing complex deep autoencoders with a lightweight, computationally efficient Isolation Forest algorithm running on a centralized Python backend that cross-references live branch power draw directly against campus academic schedules to detect and alert facility managers to off-hours ghost consumption.

- **Source Reference(s):**
  - _Section 1 & 2 (Introduction & Micro-moment Extraction, pp. 185–190):_ Mathematical formulation of energy micro-moments, extracting Device Standby Power Consumption (DSPC) and operational duration vectors to model idle versus active electrical states.
  - _Section 3 (Evaluation and Discussion, pp. 191–196):_ Experimental testing of deep autoencoder reconstruction models on building telemetry, achieving high anomaly classification sensitivity (F1-score > 98%) on idle and abnormal equipment draws.
  - _Section 4 (Conclusion & Stated Limitations, pp. 196–197):_ Explicit discussion of computational scaling constraints when deploying appliance-level neural autoencoders across large institutional infrastructures and the need for standardized lightweight monitoring.
